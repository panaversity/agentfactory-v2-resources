## The new case

"A shop owner wants an AI to delete last year's orders from her shop's system." What would you question first, and why? Lesson 3.2, "How to choose", decides it.

- **A plan passes.** The risk is a wrong action: deleting. A plan shows what the worker will delete, what it will not touch, and when it will stop, before anything happens. It also shows what the worker thinks "last year" means.
- **A dry run passes.** A deleted order cannot come back, so a mistake would be expensive. A dry run does the whole job on past data, with no real effect.
- **A plan, then a dry run, passes.** Start with the cheapest check that can show the risk.
- **A sample alone does not pass.** A sample shows the worker's judgment in a small piece of the real output. Here the risk is not judgment. It is an action that cannot be undone.
- **The reason.** It passes when it says what could go wrong: the wrong orders deleted, a deletion that cannot be undone, or the worker misunderstanding the job. "It is the cheapest", alone, does not say what could go wrong.

## Why

Once the worker starts, it works from the page, not from what you meant. So you write the four parts first (Lesson 3.2). The intent says what "done" means, and why. The why helps the worker with small choices nobody wrote down. The scope keeps the worker inside the job, so everything in Clients/Lakeview stays. The authority is about permission, not ability. A worker may be able to delete files, but Nadia keeps that for herself.

Today's date matters most. A worker with no date uses its own clock. On today's real date, four drafts in this folder, changed between Tuesday, October 7 and Tuesday, October 28, 2025, are more than a year old. On Monday, October 5, 2026, the day in Nadia's job, they are not. The same rule picks different files on a different day.

Each answer in the review contract becomes a place you look in the final 10 percent (Lesson 3.2). The stop rule makes the flags you read first. The evidence lets you spot-check. The success number is the one number you check. What must never happen automatically waits for approval. Task 3 uses all four.

Then you question what the worker made of your setup. When an answer shows a gap, the fix goes in the setup, not in the chat. The next run reads only the setup (Lesson 3.2, Step 4). You stop when the answers are boring (Step 5). Then each answer comes from a line you wrote, so you could have predicted it.

## Never a deduction

These things never make you miss a check:

- Where a fact sits. Today's date may sit under Scope or Intent. Clients/Lakeview may sit under Scope, Authority or the review contract.
- Your words, your spelling, and the order of your lines.
- More lines than this key names, such as why Clients/Lakeview stays, or what "old" means.
- A fact from Nadia's box that you added as a fix in step 9, and not in steps 1 to 5.
- An answer you judged vague that this key would call wrong, or the other way round. Both are gaps.
- How many rounds it took until the answers were boring.
- How good the plan is. Task 2 grades your setup, your lines, and where the last answers came from.
